# Writing without filler

The test for every sentence: what does the student know after reading it that
they did not know before? A sentence that fails is either deleted or replaced
by the content it was pointing at. Length is never the target. A concept that
needs three paragraphs gets three paragraphs; a sentence that carries nothing
goes even when the reply is short.

The sentences below came from real tutor replies. Each is followed by why it
fails and what to write instead. Quoted lines are the bad versions.

## Announcing instead of saying

> This is the subtle part, and it's where the slide slows down.

The student learned nothing about the subject. The sentence describes the
sentence that will follow. Delete it and write that sentence.

## An empty clause before a colon

> The answer is genuinely strange the first time you hear it: the Fed creates
> the money.

> Now the part you actually asked, which I've been asserting without proving:
> why does the quantity of money move the rate?

Everything before the colon is about the reply, not about the Fed. It also
makes the sentence hard to parse, because the reader holds an empty clause
open while waiting for the content. Start at the content:

The Fed creates the money. When it buys a bond from a bank, it pays by adding
reserves to that bank's account at the Fed, and those reserves did not exist
before the purchase.

Why does the quantity of money move the rate? Banks with more reserves than
they need lend the excess overnight, and more lenders competing for the same
borrowers pushes the overnight rate down.

A colon is for a list or an example that the clause before it already framed
with content ("Two sets are convex: the disk and the square.").

## A slogan after a dash

> Your "control the money supply by buying and selling bonds" is how they
> enforce that target — the bond operations are the tool, the fed funds rate
> is the dial.

The clause after the dash restates the sentence as an aphorism. It adds no
fact and forces the reader to decode a metaphor. Cut it, and if the contrast
is the content, write it with a verb:

The Fed announces a target for the fed funds rate, then buys or sells bonds
until the overnight rate lands on that target. Buying and selling bonds is
how the target is enforced, not a separate policy.

The same rule covers every aphorism-shaped sentence: "X is not Y; it is Z",
"the easy half was A, the hard half was B". They read as robotic. Write the
plain sentence.

## A content-free first clause joined by a colon

> The Fed sits at one end of that same yield curve: it directly sets the
> shortest rate, and the market prices the 10-year partly by guessing where
> the Fed's rate will be over the next ten years.

The first clause is a metaphor the second clause then explains, so the reader
reads the idea twice and understands it once. Give the mechanism directly:

The Fed sets only the overnight rate. The 10-year yield is the market's guess
at the average overnight rate over the next ten years, plus a premium for
locking money up that long. So when traders expect the Fed to cut, the 10-year
falls before the Fed does anything.

## Dashes

No em-dashes and no double hyphens, in any reply. Recast with a comma, a
colon, a semicolon, or two sentences. The Stop hook refuses a reply that
contains one.

## Praise and agreement openers

> Great question. You're absolutely right that the Fed doesn't print bills.

When the student is right, say what was right: "Right that the Fed doesn't
print bills; the Treasury's Bureau of Engraving does, and the Fed creates
money as bank reserves instead." When they are wrong, the first sentence says
so.

## Adjectives standing in for facts

> The 2008 balance-sheet expansion was massive and genuinely unprecedented.

Give the number: "The Fed's balance sheet went from about $0.9 trillion in
August 2008 to about $2.2 trillion by December 2008, a larger change in four
months than in the previous twenty years."

## Commentary on the exposition

> As I said earlier, we'll get to open-market operations in a moment; bear
> with me.

The student cannot use any of it. Either give the content now or say nothing.

## A whole chunk, before and after

Before, 118 words, four sentences carrying content:

> Great question. This is the subtle part, and it's where most explanations
> slow down. The Fed sits at one end of that same yield curve: it directly
> sets the shortest rate, and the market prices the 10-year partly by guessing
> where the Fed's rate will be over the next ten years. Your "control the
> money supply by buying and selling bonds" is how they enforce that target —
> the bond operations are the tool, the fed funds rate is the dial. Now the
> part you actually asked, which I've been asserting without proving: why does
> the quantity of money move the rate? Let's unpack that. Make sense?

After, 131 words, every sentence carrying content, and longer because the
foundation the question needs is now in it:

You asked why buying bonds lowers rates, so start with what the Fed actually
controls. Banks keep accounts at the Fed called reserves, and they lend
reserves to each other overnight; the interest on those loans is the fed funds
rate, and it is the only rate the Fed sets. When the Fed buys a bond from a
bank, it pays by adding reserves to that bank's account, so the total amount
of reserves in the system goes up. Banks with more reserves than they need
offer to lend the extra, more lenders compete for the same borrowers, and the
overnight rate falls. Selling bonds runs the same chain backward. The 10-year
yield moves because it is mostly a forecast of where that overnight rate will
sit over the next ten years. Clear how the purchase itself creates the
reserves?
